# What I Inherited from the Dead

Friends I have accumulated from the five countries I have lived in know different versions of me. Some know me as the girl who moved between airports and dorm rooms, who became good at introducing herself over and over again. Some know me as ambitious, busy, always trying to become someone. But the people who knew me when I was growing up in Dinagat Islands know a version of me that came before all of that. They know I was raised, in many ways, by my grandparents: my Nanay Sayong and my Tatay Pablo.

Nanay was a homemaker. Tatay was a fisherman. My mother was twenty-five when she had me: young, working, a breadwinner, and still trying to figure out the world for herself. So I spent most of my early childhood at home with my grandparents, in a life that looked ordinary from the outside but has become sacred to me now.

My upbringing was made of mundane days. Watching television with my grandparents. Listening to neighborhood gossip from women who became my extended aunts and grandmothers. Going to the farm on weekends to harvest cassava, sweet potatoes, and sometimes sugar cane. I did not know then that those days were teaching me how to see the world. I did not yet have the language for class, labor, inequality, or sacrifice. I only knew that food came from hands, that survival had a schedule, that the people who worked the hardest were rarely the ones praised for keeping the world alive.

Maybe that was the first thing I inherited from them: a way of looking.

My grandmother died when I was eleven. She was seventy-seven, and she passed away from ovarian tuberculosis. I remember it was a normal afternoon in sixth grade when my aunt came to my classroom and spoke quietly to my teacher. I was surprised to see her there. My teacher told me I should go home, and because I was a goody-two-shoes student, I simply obeyed.

On the walk home, my aunt said, “Zee, I have something to tell you.”

I had no idea what she was about to say. Not even a suspicion. Childhood protects you like that sometimes. It lets the world remain ordinary until the exact second it breaks.

When we arrived, there were already several people inside our small living room. In Filipino homes, especially when elders are present, you are expected to go around and mano — to take an elder’s hand and press it to your forehead as a gesture of respect and blessing. But that day, I could not greet anyone. I could not look at their faces. I was only looking at the white casket in the middle of the room.

There she was.

My Nanay.

I could write about the nine days that followed: the prayers, the visitors, the food, the murmurs, the strange way grief can turn a house into a public place. But what stayed with me most was not the funeral. It was my grandfather after.

Every day, Tatay visited her grave. Every single day. He missed it only when there were typhoons, and even then, as soon as it was safe, he would go back to the cemetery to clean it. He swept away leaves, branches, dirt, whatever the weather had left behind. It was not dramatic. It was not the kind of love people write songs about. No grand declarations, no speeches, no spectacle. Just an old man returning, day after day, to take care of the place where his wife rested.

That was how I first understood love.

Love, I learned, is not always loud. Sometimes love is slow. Sometimes it is gentle. Sometimes it looks like showing up to the same place every day, even when no one is watching. Sometimes it is removing debris from a grave because the person you love can no longer do it herself. Love is not only the feeling. It is the ritual. It is the maintenance. It is the refusal to let someone become forgotten just because they are gone.

Four years later, my grandfather died too. It was during the pandemic. By then, I was fifteen. His death was different, or maybe I was different. There was already a terrible familiarity to loss. We knew the rituals. We knew what had to be done. My mother could not come because of COVID restrictions and quarantine protocols, so I had to give the speech in front of our friends and family.

I do not remember everything I said. I only remember standing there and understanding, in a way I had not before, that childhood had ended in pieces. Not all at once. Not dramatically. Just through a series of moments where I had to become older than I was.

For a long time, I did not think much about their deaths. Or maybe I thought I did not. I kept moving. I went to school. I left home. I lived in other countries. I became the kind of person who could talk about grief as something that had happened years ago, something already filed away.

Then in 2023, while I was in boarding school in Japan, I was cleaning my files and found a photo of me with my grandparents on my seventh birthday. I had not seen it in years. Maybe almost a decade. In the photo, I was still small enough to believe that the people I loved would always be somewhere nearby. My grandparents were there beside me, alive in the casual way people are alive in old photographs, unaware that one day the image would become evidence.

That day, I could not get up.

Nothing new had happened. No one had died that morning. There was no phone call, no funeral, no white casket in the living room. Just a photograph. And suddenly grief, which I thought I had already survived, returned to the room and sat beside me.

Maybe this is what grief does. It does not always announce itself. It waits inside ordinary things: a photo, a smell, a song, a birthday, a food you have not eaten in years. It hides in the files you forgot you saved. It lives in the body quietly until one day it asks to be felt again.

I used to think grief was the moment of losing someone. Now I think grief is also the long after. It is realizing years later that you are still becoming a person shaped by their absence. It is hearing yourself say something your grandmother would have said. It is watching an old man clean a grave in your memory forever. It is understanding that the dead do not leave all at once.

What I inherited from my grandparents was not money, property, or anything that could be easily named. I inherited a way of paying attention. I inherited tenderness that looks like duty. I inherited the knowledge that love is not proven by intensity, but by care repeated over time. I inherited an instinct to look at who works, who eats, who gets remembered, who gets left behind. I inherited the belief that ordinary people carry extraordinary histories, even when the world does not write their names down.

And maybe most of all, I inherited the urgency to love people while they are still here.

The grind can wait. The email can wait. The task can wait. But I will pick up the phone when a friend calls. I will answer my family. I will make time for the people I love, because one day all we have left of each other are stories, photographs, rituals, and the things we wish we had said sooner.

Today is my grandmother’s birthday. She would have been eighty-six.

I love you, Nanay. I love you, Tatay.

Everything I am still learning about love, grief, work, tenderness, and survival began with you.
