If education predicts the future, then every Filipino should be worried. The
pandemic has disrupted education. The pandemic, however, did not cause the
education crisis; rather, it only exposed long-standing issues that had been holding students behind for decades. Will reopening schools make a difference?

On a Thursday afternoon in 2020, what was believed to be a one week break
for Filipino students turned into the world’s longest lockdown. On March 12, in one fell swoop, the pandemic changed the fabric of our interaction and education. And in 2022, as students across the globe brace themselves for in-person classes, the Philippines’ whopping 27 million students are staying for the second year in a row, fanning concerns about a weakening education system in a country where access to resources is uneven.

Students privileged enough to have a laptop, their own room, and a stable
internet connection may say online learning has been good for them, but the same sentiment doesn’t apply to the other side of the gap. As the labor crisis grows, so does the lack of capability for parents to financially support their children’s basic and educational needs. According to private polling firm Social Weather Stations, just over 40% of students do not have a device for remote learning. The remaining 27% used a gadget they already had, 10% were able to borrow one, and 12% had to purchase one, with families spending an average of Php8,816.00 per student. — that’s more than half the average monthly salary (Php 15,200) in the Philippines. To add, internet access remains to be a problem for both students and teachers alike. According to analysis noted by the Asian Development Bank (ADB) Thinking
Machines, sufficient internet speed in the country is at least 3.2 MBps and only 6% of the population, or 6.6 million out of 110 million, have access to it. There is even a larger gap between cities and mountainous regions and island provinces.

Further, the Department of Education initialized a modular learning system
where printed learning guides, self-learning, and parents are the main source of instruction. While deemed most accessible, this mode of learning only poses a difficulty, especially for struggling learners. This only assumes that students all have the same pace of absorbing information and have the resources to keep up with the ongoing surge of modules. Another assumption arises on the parents’ end:that parents can simply help their children with schoolwork — when in fact many parents have limited education and have little to no time to do so.

To be fair, there is one thing both privileged and unprivileged students have in
common — looming mental health concerns. According to a global school-based Student Health Survey done in collaboration with the WHO from January to March 2021, 17% of high school students age 13 to 15 years old attempted to commit suicide, 12% admitted they seriously considered attempting suicide, and 11% made plans on how they would do so.

With all these included in a long list of educational issues in pandemic, this
begs the question: Is the current education system responsive to our current needs and realities? Are students even learning?

Of course, the Philippines is only one of many countries globally grappling
with one of the worst disruptions of public schooling in modern history. Governments all over the world have struggled to balance the exigency of health and safety with the public duty to educate children. However, this is not enough reason for the Philippines to be left behind; for Filipino students to be left behind.

Neighboring ASEAN countries with relatively lower GDP and smaller
healthcare landscapes have coped well and have reopened schools. For instance, Thailand with 33,906 cases per million reopened schools by adopting blended learning where a limited number of students can meet in-person while the others can work remotely through 17 free DL TV channels; these students can then do a rotational scheme where one group is in school and the others at home. A similar scheme has also been adopted by Vietnam along with ramped-up testing, contact- tracing, and vaccination campaigns. They have also adopted a local lockdown scheme and shorter school days to decongest institutions. To put it simply, neighboring ASEAN countries have made great strides in their pandemic action and education plan. It is our time to do better for who knows what consequences loom beyond a worsening education system.

According to a UNICEF study, more than 80% of parents believe their
children’s learning improvement is slower when studying from home. Moreover, according to a case study in Siargao, the proficiency levels in math and English of elementary school students in 20 public schools are as low as 22%. Furthermore, the National Economic Development Authority (NEDA) and Socioeconomic Planning Secretary Karl Chua estimated that school closures over an extended period of time could result in productivity losses equal to $219 billion or P11 trillion over the next 40 years, corresponding to an individual’s life span in the labor force.

The pre-existing learning crisis could grow into a learning disaster without
urgent action and increased investment. Children from the most marginalized
communities pay the most price without access to excellent remote learning or even school reopening, damaging their future and perpetuating inequality.

This begs the question: What would it take to enable equal and quality
education at a mass scale? Ironically, the pandemic’s disruption has made this goal more feasible across the entire educational system. The pandemic has given us a special and specific lens on the difficulties, gaps, and inequalities in education. This lens should be used to also craft specific action plans and solutions.

First, it’s urgent to reassess how the context has changed and needs such
changes have created. For the students’ aspect, well-being, disposition, access, and engagement must be assessed. Similar features must also be assessed on the teachers’ aspect and how the current education system operates. This gives us a chance to look into current strengths, flaws, and improvement opportunities.

Second, the pandemic has highlighted the lack of interest &amp; learning in
Filipino students. Thus, reevaluating current specific learning gaps and needs in all levels using low-stakes assessments is vital. For each level, the syllabus should be changed to one that’s individualized and inclusive especially since children have varied aptitude levels. Doing so helps use the ‘differences of children’ as an advantage to increase morale, engagement, and propensity in different sectors.

Third, policies for teachers must be designed and implemented with a
systems perspective. Supporting teachers effectively requires effective, rational, and specific systems, not isolated structures, or one-off solutions. Whether it’s a new mandate on less paperwork or workshops to improve instructions & amp technical skills, effective teacher policy must be developed with clear end goals in mind, aligned, and connected to other system levers for lasting change. After all, at the end of the day, teachers are the building blocks of education.

Fourth, education infrastructures — both literal and figurative — should be
improved. Education accessibility must be prioritized especially for far-flung areas and marginalized sectors as it is the hardest yet most fundamental step in improving such infrastructure. Counseling, whether mental, behavioral, or vocational should be augmented and utilized both as a short &amp; long-term solution. The digital divide among the rich and the poor, bustling cities and mountainous areas must also be eradicated as digital knowledge accelerates the pace of learning and alters the abilities that students and workers will require in the future. Of course, extracurricular activities — ones that do not just span competitions but also mobilization opportunities - must be supported and individualized. May it be in scientific research, sports, or
community service, doing so could provide multitudes of channels for applying lessons learned in the classroom, offering students the opportunity to apply academic and holistic skills in a real-world context.

Finally, it is essential that these efforts are ushered by a vision of building
back better. Going back to the status quo only means repeating past mistakes. To reverse learning losses due to COVID-19, the state needs to focus on building better educational systems that are more equitable, effective, and resilient. To add and put it simply, school reopening is a huge undertaking but it must be done.

The state’s actions today against COVID-19 do not come without
consequences. The ongoing closure of schools costs not only us but the future
generations. With almost 2 years in lockdown, it’s time for the country to use the pandemic for our gain; to reassess, restructure, and reform. For our concern should not only be of yesterday and today, but also for tomorrow.
